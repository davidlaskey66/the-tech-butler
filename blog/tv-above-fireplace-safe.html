Mounting a TV Above a Fireplace: Is It Safe? What to Check First

Published November 24, 2026 • 8 min read

Is it safe to mount a TV above a fireplace? Often, yes — but only if three things check out: the wall above the firebox stays cool enough while the fire is running, the screen ends up at a height you can watch without craning your neck, and the wall can actually hold the mount without hitting a flue, vent pipe or gas line. If any one of those fails, the TV belongs somewhere else, or on a different kind of mount.

This question comes up constantly in Collingwood, Blue Mountains and Wasaga Beach, where so many great rooms are built around a stone or gas fireplace and the wall above it is the obvious place for a screen. Winter makes it more pressing: the fireplace runs for hours on ski weekends, and a new TV from a holiday sale is waiting in its box. Here is how to check your own fireplace wall before anything gets drilled.

Why a TV Above a Fireplace Can Be a Problem

There are three separate risks, and people usually only think about the first one.

Heat. Hot air rises off the fireplace opening and runs straight up the wall. Electronics don't like sustained heat: many TV manuals list a maximum operating temperature of around 40°C, and long exposure to high heat can shorten the life of the panel and the components behind it. Output varies a lot by fireplace type. A gas fireplace can put out 20,000 to 35,000 BTUs an hour, while many electric fireplaces produce under 5,000, so an electric insert is usually far less of a concern than gas or wood.

Viewing height. Comfortable viewing puts the centre of the screen at roughly seated eye level, around 42 inches off the floor. Above a typical fireplace and mantel, the centre of the screen often ends up 70 inches or more up the wall. That is fine for a quick glance at the hockey score and tiring for a full movie.

What's inside the wall. The space above a fireplace can hide a metal flue or direct-vent pipe, a framed chase with irregular stud spacing, or solid stone and brick that needs masonry anchors. Drilling blind into any of these is how a simple TV install becomes an expensive repair.

Step 1: Test How Hot the Wall Actually Gets

This is the easiest check and the most important one, and you can do it yourself this weekend.

  1. Tape a simple thermometer to the wall roughly where the centre of the TV would hang.
  2. Run the fireplace the way you normally would on a cold night — full flame, for several hours, not ten minutes.
  3. Check the reading. A widely used rule of thumb is that the wall should stay below about 38°C (100°F). If it climbs past that, the spot is too hot for a TV.

An infrared thermometer works too, but a taped thermometer left in place gives you a more honest picture of a long evening by the fire. Also check the TV's own manual for its operating temperature range, and read the warranty terms; some manufacturers are cautious about heat-related damage.

Step 2: Check What's Deflecting the Heat

If the wall runs warm, a mantel is your best friend. The farther a mantel projects from the wall, the more rising heat it pushes forward into the room instead of up the wall. A deep mantel can be the difference between a spot that fails the thermometer test and one that passes.

If you don't have a mantel, a non-combustible heat deflector or hood above the opening can do a similar job. Before adding anything, check your fireplace's installation manual: gas fireplaces list minimum clearances for mantels and other materials above the opening, and those numbers come from the fireplace manufacturer, not the TV installer. For wood-burning fireplaces, a common guideline is to leave at least a foot between the top of the opening and the bottom of the TV, and more is better.

Step 3: Get the Height Right (or Use a Pull-Down Mount)

Once heat is under control, height is the next question. Measure from the floor to the top of the mantel, add a few inches of clearance, and that's where the bottom of the TV will sit. If the centre of the screen lands well above eye level from your couch, you have three options:

  • Mount as low as clearances allow. Every inch helps, so don't leave a gap above the mantel just for looks.
  • Use a tilting mount. Angling the screen down a few degrees cuts glare from windows and pot lights and makes a high TV easier to watch.
  • Use a pull-down mantel mount. These sit at mantel height when not in use and pull down to eye level when you sit down to watch. The MantelMount MM540, for example, supports 44- to 80-inch TVs from 20 to 90 lb and has handles that change colour when they're hot, as a warning about heat. Expect to pay roughly $400 for that model at Canadian retailers, with simpler pull-down mounts from other brands for less. These mounts are heavy and must be anchored properly, so they're not a beginner DIY project.

Step 4: Find Out What's Behind the Wall

Before anything is drilled, you need to know what you're drilling into.

  • Drywall over a framed chase is common with gas fireplaces. Studs may not be where a stud finder expects, and the vent pipe often runs straight up behind the TV location.
  • Stone or brick can hold a TV very well, but it needs the right masonry anchors set into solid material, not the mortar joints or a thin veneer.
  • A gas line or electrical box nearby changes where you can safely drill.

Your fireplace manual and any builder drawings are worth pulling out here. If the wall is a mystery, that's a good reason to bring in a professional.

Step 5: Plan the Power and Cables

The cleanest look has no cords running down the stone. But a standard TV power cord isn't designed to be run inside a wall, and stuffing one behind the drywall is a common shortcut that shouldn't be taken. The proper options are a certified in-wall power kit, which lets you plug the TV in behind the screen without running the cord through the wall (Home Depot Canada lists kits starting around $80), or having a licensed electrician add an outlet behind the TV. HDMI and other low-voltage cables can be routed through the wall separately.

Over a fireplace, cable routing gets trickier: the chase may be insulated, the vent pipe needs its clearance, and masonry can't be fished like drywall. Sometimes the best answer is to keep the streaming box and receiver in a nearby cabinet and run just one or two cables up to the screen.

How to Prevent Problems After It's Mounted

  • Re-test after the first big fire. Feel the top of the TV and the wall behind it after a long evening. If it's hot to the touch, turn the fireplace down or reconsider the spot.
  • Watch your second home in winter. If you keep a vacation property in Blue Mountains or Wasaga Beach set cool while you're away, let the room warm up before turning the TV on. Many TVs list a minimum operating temperature around 10°C as well.
  • Keep the vents clear. Don't box the TV in tightly with trim or a recess that traps heat behind it.
  • Check the mount yearly. Pull-down and full-motion arms get used every day; make sure bolts are snug and the TV still hangs level.

When to Call a Professional

Plenty of homeowners can run the thermometer test themselves. It's time to bring someone in if:

  • The wall is stone, brick or tile, or you're not sure what's behind the drywall.
  • You want a pull-down or full-motion mount over the fireplace.
  • You want the cables hidden and need an in-wall power solution.
  • The TV is large and heavy, or the mount position is high enough that you'd be working off a ladder with a 30 kg screen.

Frequently Asked Questions

How hot is too hot for a TV above a fireplace?

A common rule of thumb is that the wall where the TV will hang should stay below about 38°C (100°F) with the fireplace running for several hours. Many TV manuals list a maximum operating temperature around 40°C, so check yours.

Is it safe to mount a TV above a gas fireplace?

It can be, if the wall passes a heat test and the mount respects the fireplace manufacturer's clearances. Gas fireplaces put out far more heat than most electric ones, so a mantel or heat deflector often makes the difference.

Is a TV above a fireplace too high to watch?

It often is. The centre of the screen ideally sits near seated eye level, around 42 inches up. A tilting mount helps, and a pull-down mantel mount solves the problem by bringing the screen down when you watch.

Can I hide the TV cables in a fireplace wall?

Usually, but not by pushing the TV's power cord inside the wall. Use a certified in-wall power kit or have an electrician add an outlet behind the TV, and route HDMI separately. Masonry and vented chases may need a different cable path.

Does mounting a TV above a fireplace void the warranty?

It depends on the manufacturer. Warranty terms vary, so read your TV's warranty and manual for any guidance on heat before mounting it over a fireplace.
